Well, it turns out that this stream of numbers directly controls the position of the cones in your speakers or headphones.

Take a look at [this YouTube video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J9fAThoyy-M), which shows the movement of a speaker cone in slow motion.

# Step 6

<script>
    import Clr from '../Clr.svelte';
</script>

When the numbers in the signal go up, the cone pushes forward.

When they go down, it pulls backward.

As the oscillator keeps updating these numbers, the cone vibrates back and forth, pushing air and creating sound waves - this is what we hear as a tone.

In previous tutorials, we learned how to use the frequency parameter of the <Clr nodeType="sine-oscillator">Sine Oscillator</Clr> to control its pitch.

This allowed us to play melodies.

Now, let's explain exactly what frequency is and what the hertz (Hz) unit signifies.

# Step 8

<script>

Take a look at the <Clr nodeType="oscilloscope">Oscilloscope</Clr> node.

Focus on the rightmost point where the displayed graph reaches the value `1`.

If you compare this moment to the movement of a clock’s second hand, you'll notice that they are in sync.

_(Give or take a small offset, since it’s difficult to start the <Clr nodeType="sine-oscillator">Sine Oscillator</Clr> and the clock at the exact same time.)_

We can see that it takes the waveform exactly one second to start at `1`, move down to `-1`, and return to `1` again.

Now, set the frequency to `2` Hz while keeping your eyes on the <Clr nodeType="oscilloscope">Oscilloscope</Clr>.

# Step 10

<script>
    import Clr from '../Clr.svelte';
</script>

As you can see, the waveform is now moving faster - twice as fast, in fact.

The <Clr nodeType="sine-oscillator">Sine Oscillator</Clr> now completes a full cycle - going from `1` to `-1` and back to `1` - two times per second.

# Step 11

<script>
    import Clr from '../Clr.svelte';
</script>

And that’s all there is to Hertz!

Hertz simply measure how many times something happens per second.

For an oscillator, this "something" is how fast it completes a cycle.

# Step 12

<script>
    import Clr from '../Clr.svelte';
</script>

Knowing that, try to imagine what will happen if you set the frequency parameter to `0.5` Hz.

Now, do it while keeping an eye on the <Clr nodeType="oscilloscope">Oscilloscope</Clr>.

# Step 13

<script>
    import Clr from '../Clr.svelte';
</script>

Since Hertz measures cycles per second, setting the frequency to `0.5` Hz tells the <Clr nodeType="sine-oscillator">Sine Oscillator</Clr> to complete half a cycle per second.

This means it takes two seconds to complete a full cycle.

So, at `0.5` Hz, the oscillator moves twice as slow as at `1` Hz and four times slower than at `2` Hz.

And that's exactly what we see on the <Clr nodeType="oscilloscope">Oscilloscope</Clr>.

# Step 14

<script>
    import Clr from '../Clr.svelte';
</script>

So far, we've only controlled the waveform along the horizontal axis.  

But we can also modify it along the vertical axis.  

To do that, set the gain parameter on the <Clr nodeType="gain">Gain</Clr> node to `0.5`.  

# Step 15

<script>
    import Clr from '../Clr.svelte';
</script>

As you can see, the waveform has lost some of its "height."  

More technically, we say that its _amplitude_ has been halved.  

Previously, the waveform had an amplitude of `1`.

Now it has an amplitude of `0.5`.  

# Step 16

<script>
    import Clr from '../Clr.svelte';
</script>

We can now see that the <Clr nodeType="gain">Gain</Clr> node performs nothing more than a multiplication by the factor set on the gain knob.  

This also explains why it results in a quieter sound.  

As we mentioned earlier, the numbers on the `Oscilloscope` represent the movement of the speaker cones.  

Intuitively, smaller movements lead to quieter sounds.
